One system, orchestrating the expertise of a full engineering team.

UnitAI runs a set of small, domain-specific language models side by side — reading camera input to detect and track objects, turning images into text, designing the hardware a sensor needs, and simulating device data to reconstruct what's missing. Each question is routed to the model built to answer it, so the result behaves like a single embedded systems engineer, not a general-purpose chatbot guessing at your datasheet.

agentic workflows

How would you automate your work with AI agents?

Start with a real task. Give an agent the right context and tools, let it work through the steps, and review the result. These concept examples explore how agents could support work beyond the chat window. They are not live UnitAI services. Open-source references are linked where a workflow draws on an existing project.

CONCEPT EXAMPLES

Six proposed workflows. Explore each example to see the steps and outputs.

01 / LOCAL BUSINESS

Find the right businesses. Start a relevant conversation.

A local agent could turn a location, a service offering, and a target business category into a shortlist of potential customers or service partners, with a tailored outreach draft for each match.

What you get

A sourced business shortlist, reasons for each match, and emails ready for review.

Explore the workflow
  1. Define the searchChoose an area, business category, and the product, advertising service, or support your company offers.
  2. Gather business contextUse permitted Google Maps / Places integrations, public business websites, or a directory you provide. Keep source links and retrieval dates.
  3. Assess the fit locallyA local model compares published business information with your offering, removes duplicates, and explains why each business may be relevant.
  4. Prepare useful outreachDraft a specific introduction using verified business context. You review the recipient and message before an authorized email integration sends it.
  5. Track the outcomeRecord approvals, replies, and opt-outs so follow-up work stays organized.
Illustrative brief

“Find businesses in Izmir that could benefit from our sensor-monitoring service. Show the evidence for each match and draft a short introduction.”

Business discovery uses public listings, not people's private location or search histories. Model inference can stay local; map lookups and email delivery require external services.

02 / PREVENTIVE HEALTH

Help care teams spot changes worth reviewing.

A proposed workflow for preventive health and insurance wellness programs could organize a participant's consented health measurements and flag changes for clinical review.

What you get

A trend summary, data-quality notes, and a review queue for the care team.

Explore the workflow
  1. Collect with permissionThe participant chooses which wearable measurements, lab results, and health records to share, and with whom.
  2. Check the dataAlign dates and units, identify missing readings, and separate unreliable measurements from usable trends.
  3. Assess risk with a validated modelA clinically validated model for the intended population would be needed to estimate specific risks. A language model can summarize the evidence and its limitations.
  4. Route to a clinicianPresent relevant trends, source measurements, and uncertainty for professional review before any care recommendation.
  5. Support follow-upAfter review, help organize agreed check-ins and reminders, with a record of who approved each action.
Illustrative output

“Some recent measurements differ from this participant's baseline. Several readings are missing. Review the source records before drawing a conclusion.”

This concept does not diagnose disease or demonstrate predictive accuracy. It supports care review, not automated insurance eligibility or pricing decisions; clinical validation is required before real use.

03 / RESEARCH

Turn a broad question into a sourced brief.

Explore a market, compare suppliers, or investigate a technical topic. A research agent could break the question into searches, follow evidence gaps, and assemble a brief you can check.

What you get

A comparison brief with source links, open questions, and a clear recommendation to review.

Explore the workflow
  1. Set the questionDefine the decision, research scope, and sources you trust.
  2. Research in roundsGenerate searches, read relevant pages, and follow up on missing or conflicting information.
  3. Build the briefCompare findings and keep citations next to the claims they support.
  4. Review the evidenceCheck source quality, dates, and unresolved questions before acting.
Illustrative brief

“Compare three sensor suppliers for an outdoor monitoring project. Include datasheets, integration constraints, and the questions we still need to ask.”

Local inference is possible with a compatible model; web research still connects to external sources.

Pattern reference: Local Deep Researcher

04 / CUSTOMER SUPPORT

Give every request the right next step.

A support workflow could recognize the type of request, retrieve relevant policy or product information, and hand the conversation to the right specialist with its context intact.

What you get

A grounded reply draft, relevant account context, and an escalation summary when needed.

Explore the workflow
  1. Understand the requestClassify the issue and identify missing information.
  2. Retrieve the factsRead approved help documents and authorized account records.
  3. Route to a specialistHand billing, technical, or order questions to the appropriate workflow.
  4. Resolve or escalatePrepare a reply; route exceptions and consequential account changes for review.
Illustrative brief

“Summarize this device setup issue, find the matching troubleshooting guide, and prepare a reply for the support team.”

This adapts a routing and handoff pattern. The reference demo uses hosted APIs; local deployment would need a compatible backend.

Pattern reference: OpenAI customer service agents demo

05 / SOFTWARE ENGINEERING

Take an issue from bug report to reviewable fix.

A coding agent could inspect a repository, reproduce a reported failure, propose a focused patch, and run relevant checks before handing the change to an engineer.

What you get

A proposed code change, test results, and a concise explanation of what changed.

Explore the workflow
  1. Read the issueInspect the relevant code, project instructions, and error logs.
  2. Reproduce and planConfirm the failure in an isolated environment and identify the smallest useful change.
  3. Implement and checkEdit the code and run appropriate tests, recording failures as well as passes.
  4. Prepare the handoffPackage the diff and validation evidence for human review before merge or deployment.
Illustrative brief

“Investigate this failing firmware build, propose a fix, and show the checks that support it.”

Firmware workflows also need the correct toolchain and, where applicable, hardware testing. A passing software test does not establish hardware correctness.

Pattern reference: OpenHands

06 / OPERATIONS

Turn scattered files into a decision-ready report.

A coordinated team of agents could read approved documents, reconcile spreadsheet information, research missing context, and prepare a weekly operations report.

What you get

An exception list, a source-linked summary, and proposed follow-up tasks.

Explore the workflow
  1. Collect the inputsSelect the reports, spreadsheets, and public sources the workflow may access.
  2. Divide the workAssign document reading, calculations, and research to suitable tools or specialists.
  3. Reconcile the resultsCheck totals and conflicting records; mark missing inputs instead of inventing values.
  4. Prepare follow-upCreate a report and action list for review before sharing or changing operational records.
Illustrative brief

“Compare this week's stock report with supplier updates and prepare a list of shortages for the operations meeting.”

An adaptation of a general multi-agent pattern, not a stock-management feature supplied by the reference project. Model and tool compatibility must be evaluated.

Pattern reference: Microsoft Magentic-One

The common pattern: gather context, plan the work, use the right tools, and make the result easy to review. References illustrate the patterns, not a UnitAI integration or endorsement. Local deployment depends on model, hardware, and tool compatibility.

how it works

One engineer's worth of expertise, split across specialized models.

A single general-purpose model has to be a little bit of everything, which means it's rarely precise about any of it. UnitAI takes the opposite approach: several small language models, each scoped to one part of the job, coordinated by an orchestrator that decides which model handles which part of a question and merges their answers into one response.

hardware

Reads component datasheets, register maps, and pinouts, and answers hardware-level questions against the actual part — not a generic guess.

firmware

Writes and reviews C/C++ firmware, RTOS tasks, and driver code grounded in the target platform's toolchain and constraints.

debug

Works through logs, error codes, and signal timing to narrow down where and why a system is failing.

docs

Turns finished work into documentation, changelogs, and handover notes a team can actually use later.

vision

Runs object detection and image understanding for camera and sensor pipelines, tuned for on-device inference.

test / qa

Generates test cases and sanity-checks generated code and answers before they reach the orchestrator's final output.

The orchestrator routes each question to the model best equipped to answer it, then folds the results together — so the system responds like one engineer who happens to know everything, instead of six separate chatbots you have to manage yourself.

process

What happens between a question and an answer.

01 — ingest

Take in the question and its context

A question, log line, or code snippet comes in — along with whatever's attached: a datasheet, an error trace, a repo path.

02 — route

Pick the right specialist

The orchestrator classifies the request and hands it to the model (or models) built to answer that kind of question.

03 — retrieve

Ground it in the real source

RAG pulls the exact datasheet page, register definition, or code section the answer needs to be grounded in — not a memorized approximation.

04 — generate

Draft the answer

The specialist model produces a draft response, code change, or diagnosis using the retrieved context as its source of truth.

05 — verify & deliver

Check it, then hand it back

The orchestrator checks the draft against the original request and returns one coherent, engineer-level answer.

focus areas

From firmware to local intelligence.

Local AI agents

Agents built for one job and tuned to a narrow domain, running on hardware you control instead of a general-purpose chat interface in the cloud.

RAG and fine-tuning

Retrieval-augmented context paired with targeted model adaptation, so answers stay grounded in your real documentation, datasheets, and code.

Embedded and IoT

STM32, ESP32, and nRF52 firmware, RTOS scheduling, and the protocols that get a device talking to the world, from sensor to cloud and back.

Edge inference

Object detection and local inference for situations where latency, privacy, or power budget rule out sending data to the cloud at all.

Dataset engineering

Building fine-tuning datasets from real source code and documentation, using self-instruct generation to scale beyond hand-written examples.

Model deployment

Quantization, GGUF conversion, and serving through llama.cpp — taking a trained model from checkpoint to a running local endpoint.
